Mock Interview — Periodic Functions & a Series of Semicircles

Two TMUA/MAT-style problems rebuilt as a rehearsal script: a functional equation that forces a triangle wave, and a curve built from infinitely many shrinking semicircular arcs. Not a real recorded interview — written for practice, read both parts aloud.

Question 1How many solutions does \(f(x)=\tfrac{x}{3}-1\) have?

Opening question

A function \(f(x)\) satisfies \(f(x)=3x\) for \(0\le x\le1\), and \(f(x-1)=f(1-x)=f(1+x)\) for all \(x\). Find the number of solutions to \(f(x)=\dfrac{x}{3}-1\).

Tutorinterviewer
Take a moment. You're told what \(f\) does on \([0,1]\), and then two functional equations that presumably extend it everywhere else. Before touching the equation \(f(x)=x/3-1\) — what should you extract from those two conditions first?
Junyoungcandidate
I should turn each condition into a geometric statement about the graph — is it a symmetry, a periodicity, something else — before I try to use it. Let me look at \(f(1-x)=f(1+x)\) first. That says the value at a distance \(x\) to the left of \(1\) equals the value at distance \(x\) to the right of \(1\). So the graph is symmetric about the line \(x=1\).
Tutorinterviewer
Good. Now the other one, \(f(x-1)=f(1-x)\). That's less obviously geometric — can you turn it into a statement about \(f\) alone, without the "\(1\)" cluttering it?
Junyoungcandidate
I'll substitute to clean it up. Let \(u=x-1\), so \(x=u+1\). Then the equation becomes \(f(u)=f(1-(u+1))=f(-u)\). So \(f(u)=f(-u)\) for every \(u\) — \(f\) is an even function, symmetric about \(x=0\).
Why the interviewer asks this way. The three-way chained equality \(f(x-1)=f(1-x)=f(1+x)\) looks intimidating as one object. Splitting it into two separate pairwise equalities and translating each into "symmetric about this vertical line" is exactly the kind of simplification interviewers want to see you do unprompted.
Tutorinterviewer
So now you have two symmetries: about \(x=0\) and about \(x=1\). What do two reflection symmetries at different axes combine to give you?
Junyoungcandidate
Composing two reflections gives a translation — by twice the distance between the mirror lines. Here the lines are \(x=0\) and \(x=1\), distance \(1\) apart, so I'd expect a shift by \(2\). Let me check it directly: \(f(x+2)=f(1+(x+1))\), and by the symmetry about \(x=1\), that equals \(f(1-(x+1))=f(-x)\), and by evenness \(f(-x)=f(x)\). So \(f(x+2)=f(x)\) — \(f\) is periodic with period \(2\).
What this checks. Recognising "reflection + reflection = translation" is a genuinely useful piece of mathematical culture, but the interviewer doesn't need you to know it by name — they need you to verify the period algebraically once you suspect it, which is exactly what happened here.
Tutorinterviewer
So: even, period \(2\), and \(f(x)=3x\) on \([0,1]\). Sketch it for me — what does the graph actually look like?
Junyoungcandidate
On \([0,1]\) it's a straight line from \((0,0)\) up to \((1,3)\), slope \(3\). By evenness, on \([-1,0]\) it's the mirror image: from \((-1,3)\) down to \((0,0)\). That's already a full period — \(2\) wide — a "tent" or "up-down" shape reaching height \(3\) at \(x=-1\) and \(0\) at \(x=0\). Then it just repeats every \(2\) units. So the graph is a triangle wave: it climbs linearly from \(0\) to \(3\) on every interval \([2k,2k+1]\) and falls linearly from \(3\) to \(0\) on every interval \([2k+1,2k+2]\), for integer \(k\). Peaks of height \(3\) sit at every odd integer, and it touches \(0\) at every even integer.
Tutorinterviewer
Now bring in the line \(y=x/3-1\). Where does it even have a chance of meeting the wave?
Junyoungcandidate
The wave only takes values in \([0,3]\), so I need \(0\le x/3-1\le3\), which gives \(3\le x\le12\). For \(x<3\) the line is negative while \(f(x)\ge0\), so there's no crossing there — except I should double check the boundary \(x=3\) itself: \(f(3)=f(1)=3\) by periodicity, but the line gives \(0\), so they don't actually meet at \(x=3\).
Tutorinterviewer
Right. So now count. What's your strategy for counting intersections on \([3,12]\) without just staring at the picture?
Junyoungcandidate
I'll go segment by segment. Each monotone piece of the wave has slope \(+3\) or \(-3\), and the line has slope \(1/3\) — much shallower — so on any single segment, \(f(x)-\text{line}(x)\) is itself strictly monotonic (it can't turn around), which means there is at most one crossing per segment. So I just need to check the sign of \(f(x)-\text{line}(x)\) at each integer breakpoint from \(x=3\) to \(x=12\), and count the sign changes.
The key idea, stated cleanly. "Difference of a slope-\(3\) piece and a slope-\(1/3\) line is monotonic ⟹ at most one root per piece" is the whole argument. Everything after this is bookkeeping.
Tutorinterviewer
Go on then — do the bookkeeping.
Junyoungcandidate
Peaks (\(f=3\)) are at the odd integers \(3,5,7,9,11\); troughs (\(f=0\)) are at the even integers \(4,6,8,10,12\). The line rises slowly through \(L(3)=0,\ L(4)=\tfrac13,\ L(5)=\tfrac23,\dots,L(12)=3\) — always strictly between \(0\) and \(3\) on the open interval, so it never sits exactly on a peak or a trough. That means \(f-L\) changes sign at every single breakpoint from \(x=3\) to \(x=12\): positive right after a peak (since \(f\) is near \(3\), line is below \(3\)), negative right after a trough. Counting the segments \([3,4],[4,5],\dots,[11,12]\) — that's \(9\) segments — each contributes exactly one crossing. Beyond \(x=12\), the line exceeds \(3\) while \(f\le3\) always, so \(f-L<0\) for good and there are no more crossings.
Tutorinterviewer
So your answer?
Junyoungcandidate
\(9\) solutions.
Why the interviewer likes this problem. It rewards exactly the habit of turning symbolic conditions into geometric ones (symmetry → periodicity → shape), and then replacing "count intersections from a sketch" with a rigorous monotonicity argument that doesn't depend on drawing an accurate picture.
Debrief — Question 1
  1. Can you convert \(f(a-x)=f(a+x)\) into "symmetric about \(x=a\)" on sight, and \(f(-x)=f(x)\) into "even function"?
  2. Do you know — or can you discover live — that two reflections about different vertical lines compose to a translation by twice the gap, and then verify it algebraically rather than just asserting it?
  3. Can you convert an abstract periodic/symmetric function with a stated formula on one interval into an explicit global description (here: a triangle wave)?
  4. Given two curves, can you replace "count intersections from the graph" with a rigorous slope-comparison argument (monotonic difference ⟹ at most one root per monotone piece)?
  5. Do you check the boundary behaviour (why no solutions for \(x<3\) or \(x>12\)) rather than just counting inside a suspected range?

Question 2Total length of a curve made of shrinking semicircles

Opening question

For every integer \(n\ge1\), define $$ f(x) = (-1)^{n+1}\sqrt{2^{2-2n}-\left(x-4+\dfrac{6}{2^{n}}\right)^{2}} $$ for \(4-2^{3-n} < x < 4-2^{2-n}\) only. Find the total length of the curve \(f(x)\).

Tutorinterviewer
This looks unpleasant symbolically. Before manipulating anything — what shape does one square-root expression like this, on its own, normally represent?
Junyoungcandidate
An expression of the form \(y=\pm\sqrt{r^2-(x-c)^2}\) is a circle of radius \(r\) centred at \((c,0)\) — the \(+\) sign gives the upper half, the \(-\) sign gives the lower half. So for fixed \(n\) I should identify \(r\) and \(c\) by matching against \(r^2-(x-c)^2\).
Tutorinterviewer
Do that matching for me.
Junyoungcandidate
Comparing \(2^{2-2n}\) with \(r^2\): \(r^2=2^{2-2n}=(2^{1-n})^2\), so \(r_n=2^{1-n}\). And the centre is \(c_n=4-\dfrac{6}{2^{n}}=4-6\cdot2^{-n}\). So for each \(n\), this is a circle of radius \(2^{1-n}\) centred at \(\left(4-6\cdot2^{-n},\,0\right)\).
Tutorinterviewer
Good. Now the domain restricts it to a semicircle — but check that the given domain actually matches the natural domain of the square root. Do the endpoints line up?
Junyoungcandidate
The square root is only real on \([c_n-r_n,\,c_n+r_n]\). Let me compute those: \(c_n-r_n=4-6\cdot2^{-n}-2\cdot2^{-n}=4-8\cdot2^{-n}=4-2^{3-n}\), and \(c_n+r_n=4-6\cdot2^{-n}+2\cdot2^{-n}=4-4\cdot2^{-n}=4-2^{2-n}\). That's exactly the given open interval \(\left(4-2^{3-n},\,4-2^{2-n}\right)\) — so nothing is being cut off or extended, it's the full semicircle with its two endpoints (where the radius meets the axis) excluded.
Why this check matters. It's tempting to jump straight to "these are semicircles" from the formula alone. Verifying that the stated domain exactly equals the circle's natural domain confirms it's a genuine, undistorted semicircle each time — not a smaller arc.
Tutorinterviewer
And the sign \((-1)^{n+1}\) in front?
Junyoungcandidate
That alternates: \(+1\) when \(n\) is odd, \(-1\) when \(n\) is even. So odd \(n\) gives the upper semicircle, even \(n\) gives the lower semicircle. The curve alternates above and below the \(x\)-axis as \(n\) increases.
Tutorinterviewer
So describe the whole curve — all \(n\) together — informally, before computing anything.
Junyoungcandidate
As \(n\) increases, the radius \(r_n=2^{1-n}\) keeps halving, and the centre \(c_n=4-6\cdot2^{-n}\) creeps towards \(4\). So it's a chain of semicircular arcs, alternating above and below the axis, shrinking geometrically, all converging in on the single point \((4,0)\) — like a spiral of shrinking bumps collapsing into one point.
Tutorinterviewer
Right — now find the total length. What's the arc length of a semicircle of radius \(r\), and how do you combine them?
Junyoungcandidate
A full circle has circumference \(2\pi r\), so a semicircle has length \(\pi r\). The total length of the whole curve is just the sum over all \(n\ge1\) of these semicircle lengths — the pieces don't overlap, they just chain together end to end — so $$ \text{Total length}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\pi r_n=\pi\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}2^{1-n}. $$
Tutorinterviewer
Evaluate that sum.
Junyoungcandidate
\(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}2^{1-n}=2^{0}+2^{-1}+2^{-2}+\cdots=1+\tfrac12+\tfrac14+\cdots\) — a geometric series with first term \(a=1\) and common ratio \(\tfrac12\), so it sums to \(\dfrac{a}{1-\tfrac12}=\dfrac{1}{1/2}=2\). So the total length is \(\pi\cdot2=2\pi\).
Tutorinterviewer
Interesting — an infinite chain of arcs with a finite total length. Does that surprise you, and can you sanity-check it a different way?
Junyoungcandidate
It shouldn't be surprising once I see the radii are geometric — the lengths shrink geometrically too, and a geometric series with ratio less than \(1\) converges, so of course the total is finite. As a sanity check: the first arc alone already has length \(\pi r_1=\pi\), and every arc after that is strictly smaller, so the total being \(2\pi\) — roughly double the first arc — feels the right order of magnitude, not absurdly large or suspiciously small.
Why the interviewer asks for a sanity check. Getting \(2\pi\) from a clean calculation is good, but interviewers specifically probe whether you have a feel for *why* an infinite process gives a finite, reasonable answer — geometric decay of the radii is the intuition that makes the convergence obvious before you even sum the series.
Debrief — Question 2
  1. Can you recognise \(y=\pm\sqrt{r^2-(x-c)^2}\) as a circle instantly, and read off the centre and radius by matching coefficients?
  2. Do you check that a given domain restriction actually equals the natural domain, rather than assuming the semicircle claim without verifying it?
  3. Can you interpret an alternating sign \((-1)^{n+1}\) geometrically (upper/lower half) rather than just carrying it as algebra?
  4. Can you convert "sum of shrinking arc lengths" into a geometric series and evaluate it with \(a/(1-r)\)?
  5. Do you sanity-check a surprising result (infinitely many arcs, finite total length) using the geometric-decay intuition, instead of just trusting the algebra?

C. Reading the key formulas aloud

SymbolSay it as
\(f(1-x)=f(1+x)\)"\(f\) of one minus \(x\) equals \(f\) of one plus \(x\)" — then immediately name it: "so \(f\) is symmetric about \(x\) equals one"
\(r^2-(x-c)^2\)"\(r\) squared minus, \(x\) minus \(c\), all squared"
\(2^{1-n}\)"two to the power one minus \(n\)"
\(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}2^{1-n}\)"the sum from \(n\) equals one to infinity of two to the power one minus \(n\)"
\(\dfrac{a}{1-r}\)"\(a\) over, one minus \(r\)" — "the sum-to-infinity formula"

D. Model script — read this aloud, then without looking

1. "First, I'll translate the two functional equations into geometric statements: \(f(1-x)=f(1+x)\) means the graph is symmetric about the line \(x=1\), and after substituting \(u=x-1\) into the other equation, I get \(f(u)=f(-u)\), so \(f\) is even."

2. "Composing a symmetry about \(x=0\) with a symmetry about \(x=1\) should give a translation by twice the gap, so I'd expect period \(2\) — and checking directly, \(f(x+2)=f(-x)=f(x)\), which confirms it."

3. "So the graph is a triangle wave: it rises linearly from \(0\) to \(3\) on \([2k,2k+1]\) and falls back to \(0\) on \([2k+1,2k+2]\), for every integer \(k\)."

4. "Since the line has slope \(1/3\) and each piece of the wave has slope \(\pm3\), their difference is strictly monotonic on every piece, so there is at most one intersection per piece."

5. "Checking the sign of \(f(x)-x/3+1\) at every integer from \(3\) to \(12\) shows it changes sign on all nine consecutive intervals, and there are no solutions outside that range — so the total is nine solutions."

6. "For the second problem, matching \(2^{2-2n}-(x-c_n)^2\) against \(r^2-(x-c)^2\) identifies each piece as a semicircle of radius \(2^{1-n}\), alternating above and below the axis."

7. "The total length is the sum of the semicircle lengths, \(\pi\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}2^{1-n}\), and since that's a geometric series with first term one and ratio one half, the sum is two — giving a total length of \(2\pi\)."

연습법: ① 소리 내어 한 문장씩 읽기 → ② 화면 가리고 도표(그래프)만 보고 말하기 → ③ 아무것도 안 보고 처음부터 끝까지 설명. 연결어(so / this gives / it follows that)를 의식적으로 바꿔가며 사용.

E. Common slips to watch for

  1. "same with" is wrong — say "the same as" ("the argument is the same as before").
  2. Don't drop the article before a countable singular noun: "an even function", not "even function"; "a geometric series", not "geometric series".
  3. integral (the object, \(\int f\,dx\)) vs. integration (the process) — don't confuse them, though this pair of problems is mostly algebra/geometry, not calculus.
  4. Don't chain everything with "so… so… so…" — rotate through this gives / it follows that / therefore / hence.
  5. If you lose the thread mid-sentence, pause and restate the full clause rather than trailing off — a complete, slightly slower sentence reads far better than an unfinished one.
